Chapter 2: The physical design

The basic components I used were:

  • A nema 17 stepper motor (like those 17 dollar ones)
  • A AS5600 magnetic encoder
  • A temu arduino uno
  • A timing belt from a broken breadmaker
  • A A4988 stepper motor driver
  • Lots of wires
  • And basic components (like LEDs, potentiometers, resistors and capacitors).

My first thought was something like this:

Initial Design concept (WIP)

Where there would be a motor (on the left) in some sort of 3D printed mount that was screwed into the base made out of a material. I ended up choosing wood for the large rectangular base. Then on the right, there would be some sort of tensioner and sitting on the motor and tensioner would be a timing belt. On the timing belt, there would be some sort of mechanism that could hold a measurement device to measure the pendulum’s tilt and move the pendulum along the belt.

  • Why did I choose a timing belt? Well, the main reason was because a bread maker that we owned just broke. I just disassembled it and I saw that it had a timing belt. Also, it’s much more precise than a regular belt (like a rubber band) and it would allow me to actually clamp something onto the belt and move it freely.

But now you might be saying, on the tensioner side, why is there a screw holding it and not the thing inside the render above? That’s because I tried it and turns out, plastic is far too weak for tensioning it so I used a metal piece instead.

However, we need to control the pendulum so I designed a clamp plate and a cover to mount an encoder to measure the stick and I thought that was that. So simple to make! (it was not).

My first design looked like this: a PLA rod with a cylinder that could rotate along with the “lid” that you see. Turns out, PLA is super light (unless you want 100% infill which is really expensive and takes days to print) and that arm was way too short. Also plastic on plastic has enormous friction so it wouldn’t even move.

clamp plate v1

Learning that, I made a new design: This “lid” and “pendulum adapter” would be held together by a bearing so that it could actually move and a welding rod so that it had weight and could actually move.

v2 with bearing

Turns out, timing belts are quite flimsy so when I put it onto my belt and added the rod, it would wobble a lot. So I moved onto my next design: The only thing that changed was the “lid” and I added a larger base so it could actually stay upright.

v3 lid with large base for balancing

Thus, it was finished so I started working on the actual measurement device which was an encoder (the AS5600).

Chapter 3: The encoder

Now, I’m going to be completely honest, just making the encoder function properly took like a month because of a few things.

Video 1.1: Sample HTML5 video playback embedded inline.

Issue 1: Encoder kept disconnecting

  • Fix: use better and tighter wires

Issue 2: Wires will come loose due to vibrations from motor

  • FIx: use hot glue to secure wires

Issue 3: wires too long because the AS5600 uses I2C

  • Fix: use pull-up resistors (calculated to be around 3.3k ohms)

Issue 4: motor makes a lot of electrical noise

  • Separate rails and add a low pass filter (use a capacitor)

Issue 5: Encoder disconnected which would brick my arduino

  • Fix: catch the errors early

Issue 6: wires too long for I2C

  • Fix: Decrease pull up resistor resistance and decrease length wherever possible

Issue 7: stepper motor produces a lot of EMI that could corrupt the signal along the way or give false readings

  • Fix: coil wires and pray (it worked)

After all these issues, I could actually read the data and perform some action that could make it stay upright
